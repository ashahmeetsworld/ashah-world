How I stopped wearing every hat myself. ͏ ͏ ͏ ͏ ͏ ͏

For a long time, every job in my brand had one person doing it. Me. Strategy, design, writing, outreach, the admin late at night. Many hats, and none of them worn well for long.

So I swapped the hats for hands.

I built a team. Not people, not yet. AI employees, each with one role, each in its own project. A creative director. A content director. One for funnels, one for clients, one that keeps the whole system in sync.

Where I started

For a long time I was hesitant about AI, especially anywhere near art. So I stopped guessing. I explored it to the very limits, then decided for myself where it belongs and where it doesn't.

Used badly, it's noise. Used with clear roles and real standards, it becomes leverage, freedom and infrastructure.

A brief, not a prompt

Every role has a brief: what it's responsible for, what good looks like, what it must never do. The content director knows my voice and my rules. The creative director knows my taste, my palette, and the things I'd never put my name to. None of them start from zero. They start from the standard.

Above them sits a small council I go to for the bigger calls, each with a different point of view, so I'm not just hearing my own thinking played back to me.

One brain

The part I'm proudest of: they all share one source of truth. When one role learns something, the others can find it. When I change a rule, it changes everywhere.

It isn't tidy

“Sometimes I get lost in one chat creating 100 things simultaneously.”

The system doesn't stop that. It just means that when I come up for air, everything I made has a place to live. And it's never finished. Every month the team gets a little sharper, because it learns what worked and what didn't.

It hasn't replaced my taste. It's made me more precise about it. You can't brief a team on a standard you can't put into words.

If you want to try it

1.Pick the one role you wish you had someone for.
2.Write its brief on one page: the job, what good looks like, what it must never do.
3.Give it your examples, not just your instructions.
4.Each week, write down one thing it got wrong, and add the fix to the brief.

That's the whole method. The team grows from there.
